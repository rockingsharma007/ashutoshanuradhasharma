There's a particular kind of question that children ask and adults stop
asking, not because they've learned the answer but because they've learned to
stop wondering. *Why is the sky blue?* is the patron saint of these questions.
Here is the answer, and it turns out to be the same answer as *why is the
sunset red* — which is the part worth staying for.

## The short version

Sunlight arrives as a mix of every color at once. When it meets the air, the
molecules scatter it — and they are far pickier about blue than red. Short
wavelengths get flung around the sky in every direction; long ones mostly
carry straight on. Look up at midday and you're seeing that scattered blue,
arriving at your eye from everywhere except the sun itself.

The effect has a name, **Rayleigh scattering**, and an almost unfairly steep
rule: the scattering scales with the *inverse fourth power* of wavelength.
Blue light, being roughly 1.7 times shorter than red, gets scattered about ten
times as hard. The sky isn't tinted blue. It's blue because blue is the light
that couldn't travel in a straight line.

## Then why does dusk undo it?

Nothing about the physics changes at sunset. What changes is the distance.

At noon the light drops nearly straight down through a thin column of air. At
dusk it comes in almost sideways, grazing through many times more atmosphere
to reach you. Over that longer path the blue is scattered away long before it
arrives — spent, given to someone else's sky — and what survives the journey
is the red and orange that never scatters easily in the first place.

> Same sun, same air, same law. The only variable is how much sky the light
> had to cross to find you. Noon and dusk are the two ends of one sentence.

It's a good thing to keep in mind, I think: that the blue overhead and the red
on the horizon aren't two phenomena but one, seen from two distances. Most
things that look like opposites are like this if you follow them far enough.
