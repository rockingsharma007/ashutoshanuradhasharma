Every so often a piece of arithmetic turns out to be a description of
character. Compounding is one of those. On paper it's just interest earning
interest. In practice it's a long argument for patience, made in a language —
exponential growth — that human intuition was never built to read.

## What it actually is

Put money to work and it earns a return. Leave the return in, and next year
*it* earns a return too. Do that for long enough and the growth stops being a
line and becomes a curve — one that stays almost flat for an uncomfortably
long time, then bends upward so steeply it hardly looks like the same graph.

That shape is the whole story, and it's why compounding is so easy to
underestimate. The early years feel like nothing is happening. The dramatic
part arrives late, and only for the people who were already there when it was
boring.

## Why time beats timing

Here is the counterintuitive result. An investor who starts ten years earlier
and then *stops* — never adding another cent — often finishes ahead of someone
who starts later and contributes far more. The early money simply had more
time to compound, and time is the one input you can never buy back.

> The best day to start was years ago. The second-best is the unremarkable
> one you're standing in now.

Which is a strange kind of comfort. It means the most valuable financial
decision most of us will make isn't a clever one. It's an early one, made
quietly, and then mostly left alone.
